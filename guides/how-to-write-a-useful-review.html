How to Write a Review That Actually Helps Someone

Last updated: September 2026

Every guide on this site depends on other people having written useful reviews. Most reviews aren't. "Great food, great service, will be back!" helps nobody, and that's the majority of what's out there.

Writing a good one takes about ninety seconds.

Name things

This is the whole trick. Specifics are what make a review useful, and vagueness is what makes one worthless.

Say what you ordered. Say what time you went. Say how long you waited. "The carnitas tacos were excellent, the fish ones were dry" tells the next person what to order. "Food was good" tells them nothing they couldn't have guessed from the rating.

The test: could this sentence be copied onto a different business's page without anyone noticing? If yes, it's not doing any work.

The one habit that matters

Mention at least one specific thing by name. Everything else is optional.

Say who it's for

A restaurant that's wrong for one occasion is perfect for another. Telling people which situation you were in makes your review usable by people in different ones.

"Too loud for a conversation but great for a group" is genuinely more helpful than four stars with no context. So is "we had a toddler with us and nobody minded," or "went alone and sat at the bar, which was comfortable."

Include the boring logistics

Nobody thinks to write these and everybody wants to know them:

  • Whether parking was findable
  • Whether you needed a reservation
  • How long the wait was, and at what time
  • Roughly what a meal cost
  • Whether the posted hours were accurate
  • Whether there were outlets, if it's the kind of place people work in

A single sentence covering two of these makes your review more useful than three paragraphs of adjectives.

When something went wrong

This is where reviews do the most damage or the most good, so it's worth being deliberate.

Say what happened, factually. "Our order took 50 minutes and arrived cold" is far more useful — and far more damaging to a business that deserves it — than "terrible service, avoid."

Say whether you gave them a chance to fix it. This is the single most valuable thing in a negative review. "I mentioned it and they remade it immediately" and "I mentioned it and was ignored" describe completely different businesses.

Consider whether it's a pattern or a bad night. Everyone has an off day. One slow evening at an otherwise good restaurant probably isn't a one-star situation, and rating it that way makes the whole system less useful for everyone.

Don't review things that aren't theirs. The weather, other customers, traffic, a delivery app's failure. It drags down a rating for something the business couldn't control.

Update your review if things change

Barely anyone does this, and it's the highest-value thing available. If a business fixed your problem, went downhill, or you've been back ten times since, edit what you wrote.

A review that says "updating a year later — still consistently good" is worth ten fresh five-star reviews, because it's evidence over time rather than a single moment.

The things to skip

  • Long descriptions of your day before arriving
  • Comparisons to a restaurant in a different city nobody's been to
  • Reviewing a place you haven't been to
  • Anything about individual staff members' appearance
  • Threats about what you'll do if the owner doesn't respond

Worth knowing: it's now illegal in the US to write a review for a business you have no experience with, or to be paid for a positive one. If a business offers you a discount in exchange for a review, that's a violation on their end.

Quick version

  1. Name at least one specific thing you ordered or experienced.
  2. Say what kind of visit it was.
  3. Include one boring logistical detail.
  4. If something went wrong, say whether they tried to fix it.
  5. Go back and update it later if things change.
