How to Eat Well in a City You've Never Been To

Last updated: September 2026

You land somewhere new, you're tired, and you're hungry. Every restaurant on the map looks equally plausible and you have no idea which street is the good one. This is where most people end up at a chain across from the hotel.

You can do better in about two minutes.

Get away from where you're standing

If you're at a hotel, near a station, or beside a famous landmark, you are standing in the worst possible place to eat. Those areas are full of restaurants that survive entirely on people who won't walk any further and will never come back.

There's no incentive to be good. Everyone's a first-time customer and there's a fresh batch tomorrow.

Moving even five or six blocks away changes the customer base to people who live there, and businesses serving locals have to earn repeat visits. That single move does most of the work.

One move, most of the benefit

Search from a point a few blocks off the tourist strip instead of from where you're standing. Everything else is refinement.

Filter hard on review count

In an unfamiliar city you have no local knowledge to fall back on, which makes review volume more important than usual. You want places with hundreds of reviews, not dozens.

A high rating on 20 reviews in a tourist area is a coin flip — it might be great, or it might be new and coasting on a few friendly early visits. A 4.5 built on 1,200 reviews is a place that has been consistently pleasing people for years. Take the boring, proven option.

Read reviews written by locals

Tap a few reviewer names. If their review history is full of places in that same city, you're reading a local. If it's a scattered trail of hotels and airports across many cities, you're reading another traveler.

Both are useful, but for different things. Locals tell you whether the food is genuinely good. Travelers tell you whether the place handles visitors well — English menus, ease of walking in without a reservation.

Search for the food, not the meal

Searching "restaurants" in a new city gives you a soup of everything. Searching a specific dish or cuisine gives you places that specialize.

Find out what the city is known for and search that directly. Somewhere known for barbecue, search barbecue rather than dinner. The specialist doing one thing for thirty years is almost always a better bet than the place with a nine-page menu.

Warning signs in an unfamiliar place

  • Someone standing outside inviting you in. Universally a bad sign. Good restaurants are busy.
  • Menus with photographs of every dish, in multiple languages, on the street. Built for tourists who don't know what they want.
  • An enormous menu. Nobody cooks fifty dishes well. It means freezers.
  • Empty at a normal mealtime while neighbors are full. Locals know something you don't.
  • Reviews that mention the view more than the food. You're paying for the view.

Green flags

  • A short menu, especially a handwritten or frequently changing one.
  • A queue of people who look like they live there.
  • Recent reviews mentioning specific dishes by name.
  • Reviews spanning years, from people who clearly keep going back.
  • It's slightly awkward to get to. Inconvenience filters for quality.

The two-minute routine

  1. Pick a point five or six blocks off the main tourist area.
  2. Search a specific cuisine or dish rather than "restaurants."
  3. Sort by rating, then ignore anything under about 200 reviews.
  4. Open the top two or three and check the newest reviews are still positive.
  5. Glance at whether reviewers seem to be local.
  6. Go to the one that's slightly harder to reach.
