Picking a Mechanic or Dentist From Reviews

It's not like picking a restaurant, and treating it the same way will burn you.

Last updated: September 2026

A bad dinner costs you $40 and an evening. A bad mechanic costs you $1,800 and a car that still makes the noise. Same star ratings, wildly different stakes — and the reviews behave differently too.

Why service reviews are a different animal

Three things change once you leave restaurants behind.

There are far fewer reviews. A busy restaurant serves 300 people a day. A good plumber might handle eight jobs. A dentist sees you twice a year. So while 40 reviews would be nothing for a taco shop, for a plumber it can represent years of steady work. Adjust your expectations down — but not all the way down. Under about 15 reviews you still don't have enough to go on.

The reviewers are angrier. People rarely write reviews about a repair that went fine. Why would they? Nothing happened. They write when they got a shocking bill or when the problem came back. This means service businesses carry more 1-star reviews than they probably deserve, and a 4.3 mechanic may be genuinely excellent.

Nobody's an expert. A diner knows whether their food was good. A patient does not know whether they needed that crown. Most service reviews are really rating communication, price, and whether people were nice — not technical competence. That's still useful. It's just not the thing you think you're reading.

Recalibrate

For service businesses, treat 4.3 as good and 4.6+ as excellent. Restaurant standards will eliminate every mechanic in your city.

Read the 3-star reviews first

This is the single best trick for service businesses, and almost nobody does it.

Five-star reviews say "great job, fast, friendly." Useless. One-star reviews are often someone furious about a price they agreed to beforehand. Also useless.

The 3-star reviews are where the honest people live. They're the ones saying "the work was solid but it took three days longer than promised" or "they fixed it right, but I had to call twice to get an update." That's the real texture of what working with this business is like.

What actually predicts a good outcome

Ignore the adjectives and hunt for these specifics:

  • The estimate matched the final bill. The number one complaint across every trade. If multiple reviews mention the quote holding, that's a strong signal.
  • They explained what was wrong. Reviewers who can describe what was actually repaired were dealing with someone who took time to explain it.
  • They said no to something. Reviews mentioning "they told me I didn't need it yet" or "they could have sold me a new one but fixed it instead" are gold. That's a business turning down money, which is the hardest thing to fake.
  • The fix held. Look for reviews updated later, or ones mentioning returning years afterward.
  • They handled a mistake well. Everyone messes up eventually. A review describing a problem that got made right tells you more than ten reviews where nothing went wrong.

Read the owner's replies

For service businesses this matters more than for restaurants, because you're going to have to talk to these people while something stressful is happening.

Look at how the business answers its worst reviews. Someone who responds with specifics and an offer to fix it is telling you how they'll treat you when something goes wrong. Someone who responds defensively, blames the customer, or argues line by line is telling you that too.

No responses at all is a neutral signal — plenty of great small operators are too busy to manage a review profile.

Watch for the volume mismatch

One specific red flag: a service business with hundreds of reviews and a suspiciously perfect rating.

A dentist seeing 30 patients a day can legitimately accumulate a lot of reviews. But a two-truck plumbing outfit with 800 five-star reviews doesn't add up — there aren't enough hours in the week. When the review count doesn't match the plausible size of the operation, something's been purchased.

Two things reviews can't tell you

Licensing and insurance. No amount of stars substitutes for checking that a contractor, electrician, or plumber is licensed in your state. That's a separate lookup, it's usually free online, and it's the one that protects you if things go badly wrong.

Price level. Reviews skew toward people who were happy to pay or shocked by the bill, with very little in between. Get a written estimate. For anything over a few hundred dollars, get two.

MyTopRate covers mechanics, dentists, plumbers, and electricians alongside restaurants, and the Recommended sort is especially useful here — it stops a brand-new shop with four glowing reviews from outranking the one that's kept a 4.5 across two hundred jobs.

Quick checklist

  1. Reset your standards: 4.3+ is good for service businesses.
  2. Skip to the 3-star reviews and read them all.
  3. Look for mentions of estimates holding firm.
  4. Check how the owner replies to bad reviews.
  5. Verify licensing separately, and get it in writing before work starts.
