What the $ $$ $$$ Symbols Actually Mean

Last updated: September 2026

Those little dollar signs look like a precise scale. They're closer to a rough vibe, and misreading them is how people end up surprised by a bill.

They're relative, not absolute

The single most important thing to know: the symbols compare a business to other businesses in the same area, not to a fixed national price list.

A $$ restaurant in midtown Manhattan and a $$ restaurant in rural Ohio are not remotely the same price. Both are "moderately priced for around here." Around here differs by a factor of three.

This matters most when you travel. Your instinct for what $$ costs is calibrated to home, and it doesn't transfer.

Rough guide, US restaurants

$ = under about $10 a person · $$ = roughly $10–25 · $$$ = roughly $25–50 · $$$$ = above that. Treat these as loose. In expensive cities, shift every band upward.

Where the rating comes from

It's not audited. Price level gets set from a mix of what the business itself indicates, information on its menu and website, and signals from users who've been asked about it. Nobody is checking receipts.

Which means it drifts. A place that opened cheap and gradually raised prices over four years may still be showing the symbol it earned in year one. Menu prices have moved a lot in recent years, and price symbols have not kept pace.

What the symbol doesn't capture

  • Drinks. The food might be $$ while the wine list is emphatically not. Two cocktails can double a bill and won't show up in the symbol at all.
  • What a full meal actually costs. A place with cheap tacos looks like $, but if a filling meal takes four of them plus a drink, you're at $$ prices.
  • Whether it's worth it. Price level says nothing about value. A $$$$ restaurant can be a bargain and a $ one can be a waste.
  • Lunch versus dinner. Many restaurants are two different price tiers depending on the hour, and only one symbol gets shown.

How to find the real number in 20 seconds

Skip the symbol and look at the menu. Almost every listing links to one, and if it doesn't, the customer photos usually include somebody's snapshot of a physical menu.

Find one main dish you'd plausibly order and look at its price. That single number tells you more than the symbols ever will. Add a drink and roughly 25% for tax and tip and you have a real per-person estimate.

The other reliable source: reviews frequently mention prices, usually when someone thought they were too high. Searching a listing's reviews for "expensive" or "price" surfaces those quickly.

The useful way to use the symbols

They're bad at telling you what something costs and quite good at one narrow job: filtering out things in the wrong category entirely.

If you want a quick weekday lunch, ruling out $$$$ saves you time. If you're planning a proper dinner, filtering out $ avoids a list full of counter-service spots. Use them to set the boundaries of the search, then check actual menus for anything you're seriously considering.

MyTopRate deliberately sorts on rating rather than price, because a cheap bad meal isn't a bargain. Every result links straight to its Google listing, where the menu and its real prices are one tap away.

Quick version

  1. The symbols are relative to the local area, not a fixed scale.
  2. They're often out of date, and they lag price increases.
  3. They ignore drinks, which is where bills actually inflate.
  4. Look up one main dish on the menu — that's your real signal.
  5. Use symbols to narrow the field, not to predict the bill.
