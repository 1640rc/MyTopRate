Read the Owner's Replies, Not Just the Reviews

Last updated: September 2026

Reviews tell you what happened to other people. Owner responses tell you what happens next — which is the part that affects you if your visit goes sideways.

Scroll to the 1- and 2-star reviews and read what the business wrote underneath. It takes a minute and it's remarkably revealing.

What a good response looks like

The pattern is simple: acknowledge the specific problem, take responsibility, offer to make it right, and do it without arguing.

"You're right that the wait was unacceptable on Saturday — we were short two servers and handled it badly. Please email us, we'd like to have you back." That's a business that knows what happened and isn't pretending otherwise.

The tell is specificity. A response that references the actual visit means somebody looked into it.

What you're really testing

Not whether they're polite. Whether they take responsibility when it costs them something.

The bad patterns

Arguing with the customer

Point-by-point rebuttals, corrections about what "really" happened, or explanations of why the customer was wrong. Even when the business is right, this tells you exactly how a disagreement with you would go.

Blaming the customer's expectations

"If you had read the menu you'd know..." or "we're not that kind of restaurant." Sometimes fair, but a business that reaches for this repeatedly has decided its customers are the problem.

Identical copy-pasted replies

The same paragraph under twenty different complaints. Nobody read anything. It's a task someone completes rather than a problem anyone solves — and it means a real complaint from you would get the same treatment.

Only answering the good ones

Warm thank-yous under every 5-star review and silence under every 1-star. That's reputation management, not customer service. It also means the negative reviews were probably accurate enough that there was nothing to say.

Getting personal

Naming the customer, speculating about their motives, or suggesting they're a competitor. A business that behaves this way in public is telling you something important for free.

What silence means

No responses at all is genuinely neutral, and worth saying clearly because people over-interpret it.

Plenty of excellent small businesses never touch their listing. The owner is cooking, not managing a review profile. A busy independent restaurant with no replies and a 4.6 is not a warning sign.

It matters more for service businesses — a mechanic or contractor who ignores complaints publicly may well ignore yours privately.

Where this matters most

For a coffee shop, skip it. The stakes are two dollars and you'll never need customer service.

For anything where things can go meaningfully wrong — car repair, home contractors, dentists, movers, wedding vendors, hotels — this check is worth more than the star rating. Every one of those transactions has a real chance of a problem, and the response pattern is your best available preview of how that gets handled.

Every result on MyTopRate links to its full Google listing, which is where responses live. Once you've picked a mechanic or contractor from the ratings, spend one more minute reading how they answer their critics before you call.

Quick version

  1. Go straight to the 1- and 2-star reviews.
  2. Read what the business wrote underneath.
  3. Specific and accountable is good. Defensive or copy-pasted is bad.
  4. Only replying to praise is worse than replying to nothing.
  5. Weight this heavily for expensive services, lightly for cheap ones.
