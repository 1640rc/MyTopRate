Choosing a Gym or Salon

Two categories where the reviews are mostly about something other than the service.

Last updated: September 2026

Gyms and salons both involve ongoing relationships rather than one-off transactions, and their reviews get distorted in specific, predictable ways. Knowing how changes what you look for.

Gyms: the ratings are mostly about billing

Read enough gym reviews and you notice something. Very few of them are about exercising. They're about contracts, cancellation, and getting charged after leaving.

This makes sense — nobody writes a review about a normal workout, but everybody writes one when they've been billed for four months after they thought they'd quit. So gym ratings end up measuring the front desk and the contract terms far more than the facility.

That's not useless. Billing disputes are the most common way a gym membership goes wrong, and a place with a wall of cancellation complaints is telling you something real about how it operates.

Gym-specific search

Search the reviews for "cancel." If that word appears constantly and angrily, the ratings are warning you about the paperwork, not the treadmills.

What actually matters for a gym, and where to find it

  • Crowding at your hours. The single biggest factor in whether you'll keep going. Check "popular times" for the hours you'd actually attend, and search reviews for "crowded" or "busy" — people specify times.
  • Cleanliness. Reviews are reliable here; it's visible and people mention it.
  • Whether equipment is broken. Recurring complaints about the same broken machines across months means nobody's fixing anything.
  • The specific thing you need. Squat racks, a pool, classes, childcare. Search reviews for it rather than assuming.

And go visit. Gyms almost universally offer a free trial or day pass, which makes reviews far less necessary than in categories where you can't sample first.

Salons and barbers: the rating is about individuals

Completely different distortion. A salon's overall rating averages together stylists who may have wildly different skill levels. The 4.5 tells you very little, because you're not booking the salon — you're booking one person in it.

Which is why the useful move is to read reviews for names. Most salon reviews name the stylist, and a pattern emerges fast: several people raving about the same person, others disappointed by someone else.

Find the name that keeps coming up positively and request them specifically when you book. That single step matters more than which salon you choose.

Other salon-specific signals

  • Customer photos are a portfolio. This is one of the few categories where photos show actual results. Look at them properly.
  • Look for your hair type or service. A salon can be excellent at one thing and inexperienced at another. Search reviews for curly, color, fade, balayage — whatever applies to you.
  • Price complaints matter more here. Salon pricing is often quoted low and lands high once add-ons appear. Repeated surprise-cost complaints are a real flag.
  • Stylist turnover. If reviews rave about someone who left, those reviews are worthless to you. Check whether recent reviews mention the same names as older ones.

What both categories share

They're recurring commitments, which changes the calculus. A bad restaurant costs you one evening. A bad gym costs you a year of contract, and a bad haircut costs you six weeks of looking at it.

So it's worth more research than a one-off purchase, and worth prioritizing consistency over peak quality. A reliable 4.4 you'll visit for years beats a 4.8 that's inconsistent.

Quick version

  1. Gyms: search reviews for "cancel" and check popular times for your hours.
  2. Gyms: take the free trial — it beats any review.
  3. Salons: read for stylist names and book a specific person.
  4. Salons: treat customer photos as the portfolio they are.
  5. Both: favor consistency, because you're committing to repeat visits.
