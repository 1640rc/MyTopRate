How to Spot a Fake Review in About 30 Seconds

Last updated: September 2026

A place has 4.9 stars and glowing reviews, and something about it feels off. Trust that feeling. Fake reviews are common enough that knowing the tells is genuinely worth 30 seconds of your time.

The good news: fake reviews are lazy. Whoever writes them is doing it in bulk, and bulk work leaves fingerprints. Once you know what to look for, they're hard to miss.

First, check the timing

This is the single fastest tell. Sort the reviews by newest and look at the dates.

Real reviews trickle in. A few this week, a couple last week, some from last month. That's what it looks like when actual customers wander in and out of a business.

Fake reviews arrive in clumps. Fifteen five-star reviews across three days in March, then silence until August. Nobody has a March like that. What you're seeing is somebody buying a batch.

Watch especially for a burst of glowing reviews right after a cluster of angry ones. That's a business trying to bury a bad stretch rather than fix it.

30-second version

Sort by newest. If a pile of 5-star reviews all landed within a few days of each other, be skeptical of the whole rating.

Then read how they're written

Real reviews are specific and a little messy. People mention the thing they ordered, the server's name, the fact that parking was a nightmare, how their kid spilled something. They complain about one detail while praising everything else.

Fake reviews are smooth and empty. They sound like this:

  • "Great service and amazing atmosphere. Highly recommend!"
  • "Best experience ever. The staff was very professional."
  • "Absolutely fantastic. Will definitely be coming back."

Nothing in there proves the writer was ever in the building. No dish, no detail, no moment. Those sentences would fit a dentist's office as easily as a taco shop.

Another tell: repeated use of the business's full name. Real people say "this place" or "we went here." Fake reviews say "Joe's Premium Auto Repair of Downtown Miami was excellent," because they're written to help the business show up in searches.

Click the reviewer's name

This takes five seconds and settles most cases. On Google, tapping a reviewer's name shows you everything else they've reviewed.

Warning signs on that profile:

  • Only one review, ever. One is not damning on its own — everybody starts somewhere — but a rating held up mostly by one-review accounts is a real problem.
  • Reviews scattered across cities they'd never plausibly visit. A nail salon in Phoenix, a mover in Newark, and a dentist in Tampa, all five stars, all in the same month.
  • Every review is a 5 or a 1. Real people give out plenty of 3s and 4s.
  • No profile photo, or a photo that looks like a stock headshot.

Don't forget fake negative reviews

Not all fakes are flattering. Competitors sometimes leave bad reviews to drag a rival down, and disgruntled ex-employees do it too.

The tells are similar but inverted. A sudden burst of 1-star reviews with no specifics, complaining about vague things like "terrible service" without ever describing what happened. Or several one-review accounts posting the same complaint within a few days.

A useful sanity check: read how the owner responds. A real complaint usually gets a response referencing an actual visit. A fake one often gets "we have no record of this customer" — and that response is frequently telling the truth.

The law changed, but it hasn't fixed things

In October 2024, the Federal Trade Commission's rule banning fake reviews took effect. It's now illegal in the US to buy or sell fake reviews, to write reviews as an insider without disclosing it, or to use AI to generate reviews from people who don't exist. Businesses also can't suppress honest negative reviews. Penalties run to tens of thousands of dollars per violation.

That's real enforcement power, and it has pushed some of the worst operators out. But enforcement is slow and the internet is enormous. Plenty of fake reviews are still sitting there right now, which is why the manual checks above still matter.

If you're confident you've found bought reviews, you can report it to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov, and flag the individual review through Google directly.

The shortcut: volume protects you

Here's the practical reason review count matters so much. Faking a rating is cheap when a business has 12 reviews — twenty purchased ones will completely rewrite the score.

Faking a rating with 3,000 real reviews is a different job entirely. Twenty fakes move a number like that by almost nothing. Big review counts are expensive to manipulate, which means they're usually honest.

This is part of why MyTopRate's Recommended sort weighs review count alongside the rating — the places that are easiest to fake are exactly the ones a raw star ranking pushes to the top.

Your quick checklist

  1. Sort by newest and look for suspicious clumps of dates.
  2. Read three reviews — do they mention anything specific?
  3. Tap two reviewer names and look at their history.
  4. Check whether the good reviews arrived right after bad ones.
  5. When in doubt, trust the place with thousands of reviews over the place with twenty.
