A 4.2 Taco Truck Can Beat a 4.8 Steakhouse

Last updated: September 2026

Star ratings feel like a universal scale. Four stars here, four stars there, same thing. They're not. A rating only means something next to other places of the same kind, and comparing across categories quietly leads you wrong.

People rate against expectations, not against perfection

Nobody walks up to a food truck expecting linen napkins. They expect good tacos, fast, for not much money. Deliver that and you get five stars, because you did exactly what you promised.

Walk into a restaurant charging $95 a head and the standard shifts completely. Now the bread matters. The pacing matters. The server's attitude matters. There are twenty ways to fall short that a taco truck was never being judged on.

So a 4.6 taco truck and a 4.6 tasting menu are not equivalent achievements. The second one cleared a much higher bar with far more ways to fail.

The core idea

The more a place costs and the more it promises, the harder every star becomes. Compare within a category, not across.

Roughly what to expect by category

These aren't hard numbers, but the pattern holds up almost anywhere:

  • Casual, cheap, specialized — taco trucks, bakeries, coffee shops, ice cream. Often 4.5 to 4.8. Simple promises, easy to keep.
  • Mid-range sit-down restaurants — usually 4.0 to 4.5. More moving parts, more chances to disappoint.
  • Fine dining — often 4.3 to 4.7, and every tenth of a point is hard-won against very demanding reviewers.
  • Fast food — frequently 3.5 to 4.0, largely because of drive-through waits and order accuracy rather than the food.
  • Bars and nightlife — all over the place, because "good night" is subjective in a way "good sandwich" isn't.
  • Medical and dental — often lower than you'd expect. People review the bill and the wait as much as the care.
  • Auto repair, moving companies, contractors — 4.0 to 4.5 is strong. These are stressful, expensive transactions and reviewers are unforgiving.
  • Hotels — usually lower than restaurants overall, because one bad night ruins the whole review.

Price changes everything

There's a rough rule worth carrying around: the higher the price, the harsher the review.

Spending $12 and getting something decent feels like a win. Spending $200 and getting something merely decent feels like being robbed. Same food quality, opposite emotional reaction, and reviews measure the emotional reaction.

This means an expensive place holding 4.5 across a lot of reviews is doing something quite impressive. And a cheap place at 4.5 is fine but unremarkable for its category.

Location shifts the baseline too

Ratings drift by area, not just category.

In a city with a serious food culture, competition is fierce and diners are picky — 4.3 might be a very good restaurant. In a small town with six options, the best place in town might sit at 4.7 simply because there's less to compare it to.

Tourist areas skew low. Lots of one-time visitors, unfamiliar expectations, higher prices near attractions, and nobody has any loyalty to the place. Airports, train stations, and theme park areas are the extreme version.

How to actually compare

  1. Search one category at a time. Compare pizza to pizza. Mixing categories in one list makes the numbers meaningless.
  2. Look at the spread in your results. If everything nearby is between 4.0 and 4.4, then 4.4 is the local best — don't hold out for a 4.8 that doesn't exist in that area.
  3. Weight price into the reading. An expensive place at 4.5 is better than a cheap place at 4.6.
  4. Read a couple of reviews to see what people expected. That tells you which scale you're looking at.

This is exactly why MyTopRate's Recommended score is calculated fresh for each search. It compares the places in your results against each other — the local, same-category peer group — instead of against some fixed universal standard that doesn't exist.

The practical upshot is simple: stop asking whether 4.4 is a good rating. Ask whether it's a good rating for this kind of place, in this area, at this price. That question has a useful answer.
