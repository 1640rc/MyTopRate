Should You Try a Place With Only 12 Reviews?

Last updated: September 2026

Everything else on this site tells you to trust review volume. So what do you do with the new place down the street that has fourteen reviews and a 4.9?

Sometimes you go. Here's how to decide.

Why the number is meaningless but the reviews aren't

A 4.9 built on fourteen reviews tells you essentially nothing as a score. The sample is too small, and early reviews skew friendly — they're often friends, neighbors, and people excited about something new opening nearby.

But the text of those fourteen reviews is still real information. You just have to read them instead of trusting the average. With fourteen reviews, you can read all of them in three minutes. With three thousand you never could.

That's the trade. You lose the statistical shortcut and gain the ability to read the whole record.

The shift

For established places, trust the number and skim. For new places, ignore the number and read everything.

What to look for in early reviews

  • Specific dishes named repeatedly. If four people independently mention the same thing being excellent, that's a real signal even from a small sample.
  • Mentions of the owner or chef being present. New places live or die on whether someone who cares is in the building.
  • Honest notes about growing pains. "Service was slow but the food was worth it" is a more trustworthy review than five words of praise. It suggests a real customer, and it tells you what you're signing up for.
  • Reviewers with history. Tap a couple of names. Locals with dozens of reviews across the area carry more weight than accounts created last week.

Warning signs specific to new businesses

  • All fourteen reviews posted within the same week. Especially at opening. That's either an organized push or actual friends, and neither predicts your experience.
  • Every review is exactly five stars with no detail. Real early customers notice things. Perfection with no texture is suspicious at any review count.
  • Complaints about basics. Wrong orders, long waits, and confused staff are normal in week one and much less forgivable in month six. Check the dates.
  • Reviews that mention a discount for reviewing. That's against the rules and tells you the rating was manufactured.

When it's worth the gamble

Some situations genuinely favor the new place:

  • Low stakes. A $14 lunch on a normal Tuesday. Worst case you had a mediocre sandwich and learned something.
  • It's a specialist. A place doing one thing — one cuisine, one product — is a better bet than a new place with a sprawling menu.
  • The team came from somewhere good. Reviews or the business description often mention this. A chef with a track record is worth more than fifty reviews.
  • You want it to survive. This is a legitimate reason. New independent businesses fail constantly, and early customers are the difference. If you want good things near you, occasionally you have to fund them.

When to wait

Skip the gamble when the visit matters: an anniversary, a group where someone will be annoyed, a client dinner, or anything where you can't absorb a bad outcome. Also skip it for expensive meals. Losing $30 on an experiment is fine. Losing $200 is a different feeling.

For service businesses — mechanics, dentists, contractors — wait longer. The stakes are higher and the downside is worse than a disappointing dinner.

This is the one case where MyTopRate's Recommended sort will actively work against you, and deliberately so — it pushes low-review places down because that's the right default. If you're specifically hunting for something new, sort by Top Rated and look at what has a high rating with few reviews.

Quick version

  1. Ignore the average entirely under about 30 reviews.
  2. Read every review — it only takes a few minutes.
  3. Look for the same dish praised by different people.
  4. Check whether the reviews arrived all at once.
  5. Gamble on cheap and low-stakes; wait on expensive and important.
